# The Third Row: A Memoir

## By Saurabh Kumar Dey

**The Third Row: A Memoir** is a contemporary literary memoir by Indian author **Saurabh Kumar Dey**.

A raw and honest personal narrative about growing up in a small town, chasing big dreams, facing rejection, navigating love and family, losing a defining ambition, and eventually discovering that your worth is not determined by your title, your relationships, or the row you are allowed to sit in.

> “Some seats are reserved. Some are earned. Some you stop needing.”

## About the Book

*The Third Row: A Memoir* is an unsparingly honest personal narrative charting ambition, emotional rescue, relationships, family sacrifice, institutional status, heartbreak, and the quiet journey back to oneself.

The story follows Saurabh Kumar Dey from his childhood in Varanasi through his mid-twenties.

At the center of the memoir is a recurring question:

> **What happens when the titles, dreams, relationships, and identities you built your life around begin to disappear?**

The book examines what remains when the external structures through which a person understands their own worth are stripped away.

---

## The Story Behind the Title

The memoir begins with a childhood experience during an Independence Day function inside the **Banaras Locomotive Works railway colony**.

As a child, Saurabh was sitting in the third row when he was told to vacate his seat because the first two rows were reserved for “officers.”

The incident was eventually resolved through his father's position and connections, but the experience left behind a question that would follow him for years:

**Who are these people, and what exactly makes them worthy of the deference society gives them?**

Years later, another incident on a train would reinforce the same question.

While travelling with a confirmed 2AC ticket, Saurabh encountered a ticket examiner who questioned whether he had the status to travel in 2AC. A phone call involving a railway connection changed the examiner's behaviour almost immediately.

The experience revealed the central tension of the memoir:

**People often respond not to who you are, but to what they believe your position, name, title, or connections mean.**

The third row therefore becomes more than a childhood memory.

It becomes a metaphor for social hierarchy, institutional status, identity, and self-worth.

# About the Story

The memoir unfolds through several interconnected stages of Saurabh's life.

## Childhood & Mentorship in Banaras

The story begins in Varanasi, within the culture of the railway colony.

It explores academic pressure, teenage rebellion, family dynamics, the quiet suffering of a self-sacrificing mother, and the influence of a physics teacher named **Shakir sir**, who becomes an important intellectual anchor.

---

## The Dream That Died Six Months Early

One of the defining experiences of the memoir is the collapse of Saurabh's lifelong ambition to join the **Armed Forces**.

The dream does not disappear because of a lack of preparation.

Instead, it is disrupted by an administrative mismatch in official date-of-birth paperwork.

A bureaucratic error effectively deletes the future he had built his adolescence around.

The memoir explores what happens psychologically when a person loses not merely a goal, but an entire imagined version of themselves.

---

## Flute: The Education in Attachment

The book then moves into an intense relationship that begins over a game and develops across distance.

The relationship explores longing, guarded emotions, pride, attachment, and the early development of emotional dependence.

It becomes an important part of the narrator's understanding of love and the difference between intimacy and needing someone.

---

## Mogli & The Rented House

Another major relationship introduces the memoir's exploration of the **rescuer complex**.

Saurabh becomes deeply involved in the life of a withdrawn classmate, gradually taking on the role of protector.

The relationship eventually develops into a shared domestic life in a rented house.

In the process, financial resources, emotional boundaries, personal safety, and identity begin to blur with the desire to protect another person.

The memoir examines the uncomfortable realization that:

**Giving everything to someone is not necessarily the same thing as loving them.**

---

## The General-Class Ticket to Kolkata

The narrative reaches a point of emotional reckoning.

A period of withdrawal, unanswered calls, an urgent journey across state lines on an unreserved train, and a confrontation with a friend force the narrator to confront what has happened to his dignity and sense of self.

The journey becomes an important turning point.

---

## Sitting in the Third Row Without Apology

The final movement of the memoir returns to family, personal responsibility, unemployment, and self-reflection.

Facing his mid-twenties without the titles, ambitions, relationships, or savior roles that once defined him, the narrator begins to discover a different kind of self-worth.

One that cannot be granted by an institution.

One that cannot be withdrawn by another person.

One that does not depend on being needed.

And one that does not require a front-row seat.

# Core Themes

## 1. The Third Row Metaphor

The memoir examines how society can defer to titles, designations, ranks, surnames, and proximity to power rather than human worth.

It asks what happens when we build our own sense of value around whether institutions and other people allow us to sit in the front.

---

## 2. The Dream That Disappeared

The book explores the psychological impact of losing a lifelong ambition.

When a future disappears unexpectedly, the loss is not simply about the goal itself.

It can also mean mourning the person you believed you were going to become.

---

## 3. Love vs. Needing to Be Needed

The memoir examines the blurred boundary between genuine intimacy and the psychological need to become indispensable to another person.

It explores the possibility that being needed can sometimes become a substitute for feeling inherently worthy.

---

## 4. Karna's Armor & Sacrificial Giving

Drawing on the metaphor of **Karna**, the memoir reflects on sacrifice and the tendency to give away financial stability, emotional boundaries, time, and energy in the belief that rescue is equivalent to love.

It questions the cost of continually bleeding for people who never asked you to.

---

## 5. What Remains

The final question of the memoir is simple but difficult:

**When the uniforms, designations, relationships, ambitions, and savior roles disappear, what is left?**

The book explores the possibility that what remains is not emptiness, but a quieter and more grounded sense of self.
